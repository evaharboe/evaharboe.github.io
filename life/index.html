Yoga

I did my 200-hour yoga teacher training at House of Om in Ubud, Bali, a mix of vinyasa and hatha with some ashtanga and meditation. Now I teach weekly classes for the Surrey Yoga Society, where I was president for two terms. Classes are open to complete beginners, and the focus has always been on making them feel like a community. Attendance doubled in the year I started teaching.

My classes are usually rocket or power vinyasa, and sometimes slower stretching sessions with more of a yin feel. Lately I've been leaning towards yoga for runners: mobility, strength and recovery for people who spend a lot of time on their feet.

Retreats

Retreats are where it all comes together. I've planned and led three for the yoga society, in England and Cyprus, and these are the kinds I'd love to run next. The active ones keep the yoga to a morning and an evening session, so the day is free for the activity.

  1. Run & yogaMorning flow, a run in the middle of the day, and a slow stretch in the evening. One workshop on mobility for runners.
  2. Hike & yogaMorning flow, a long day in the mountains, and an evening session to recover.
  3. Ski & yogaMorning flow, a day on the mountain, and a long evening stretch for tired legs.
  4. Golf & yogaMorning mobility, a round in the afternoon, and an evening stretch. For people who'd rather not wait until retirement to take up golf.
  5. Pure yogaThree sessions a day, with workshops like handstands and inversions, and plenty of time to rest in between.

About me

I was born in China and grew up in Stavanger, on Norway's west coast, in a Norwegian-French family. Most weekends we'd head up to the family cabin to ski in winter and hike the rest of the year, so being outside was never something I had to plan. It was just what we did.

Sport was the other constant. I tried almost every sport going, from swimming and climbing to dance and martial arts, but handball was the one I stuck with, from Year 5 to about Year 10. Yoga came in almost by accident, as a way to get more flexible for matches. Somewhere along the way that flipped: the yoga stayed and the handball didn't.

Then I moved to England for university, and the mountains were suddenly a long way off. Surrey has hills, but they barely count. So I started running, mostly as an excuse to get outside, and soon found that the slower and longer I went, the more I enjoyed it. When you're not chasing a pace, you actually notice where you are. That's how a way to get some fresh air turned into training for a marathon.

Travel has run alongside all of it. I've been to 35 countries, many of them on my own, and what stays with me is never really the sights. It's the people I met and the time we spent together. That's what I want retreats to be: a group of people somewhere beautiful, moving together and leaving with good memories.
